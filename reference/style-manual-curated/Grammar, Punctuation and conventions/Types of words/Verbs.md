# Verbs

Verbs express when something happened, or that something is continuing or finished. Verbs help people make sense of other parts of a sentence or clause.

---

## Guidance

- Verbs describe an action, a state, an event or a change  
- Verbs must ‘agree’ with the subject  
- Objects complement some verbs  
- Tense changes the form of the verb  
- The ‘mood’ of a verb conveys meaning  
- Phrasal verbs need a preposition or adverb  

---

## Verbs describe an action, a state, an event or a change

Verbs are words that describe:

- an action  
- a state  
- an event  
- a change.  

**Example**  
> to work, to discuss, to try [actions]  
> to be, to have, to seem [states]  
> to happen, to occur, to result in [events]  
> to become, to grow, to dissolve [changes]  

The form of the verb changes depending on the grammatical subject and tense.

---

## Verbs must ‘agree’ with the subject

The form of the verb changes to show whether the subject is singular or plural (the number). This is called *subject–verb agreement*.

**Correct**  
> The analyst works in the office on Mondays. [There is one specific analyst, so the verb takes the singular form ‘works’.]  

**Incorrect**  
> Jan work in the office on Mondays. [There is one person, Jan, so the verb should not take the plural form ‘work’.]  

To decide which form of the verb you need, find its subject and ask ‘who’ or ‘what’ is doing the verb.

**Example**  
> Question: Who works in the office on Mondays?  
> Answer: The analyst.  

Subject–verb agreement is not always intuitive. A common mistake is to use the plural form of a verb with a collective noun.

Sentences contain at least a subject and a verb. Keep the relationship between the grammatical subject and the verb clear to users. This is the basis for writing plain language sentences.

## Objects complement some verbs

Some verbs need an object to have meaning. These are *transitive* verbs.

**Example**  
> They need advice before the hearing. [‘Advice’ is the object of the verb ‘need’.]  
> Take my advice. [‘My advice’ is the object of the verb ‘take’.]  

Some verbs don’t have an object. These are *intransitive* verbs. Intransitive verbs can have a complement in the form of an adverb or adverbial phrase.

**Example**  
> The power grid failed. [The verb ‘failed’ has no object.]  
> The candidate campaigned tirelessly. [‘Tirelessly’ is an adverb complementing the verb.]  

Some verbs can be transitive or intransitive.

**Example**  
> They opened the door. [The object is ‘the door’.]  
> The door opened. [No object.]  

A complement can also link the subject to an attribute, quality or characteristic. This is not an object but completes the sentence, often as an adjectival phrase or adjective.

**Example**  
> The door is open. [‘Open’ is an adjective complementing ‘is’.]  
> The forecasts seem reasonable. [‘Reasonable’ complements ‘seem’.]  
> Each agency has its own arrangements. [‘Its own arrangements’ complements ‘has’.]

## Tense changes the form of the verb

Verb form changes to show:

- when something happened (past, present or future) – this is the tense  
- whether something is continuing or has finished.  

**Example**  
> I was writing the report last week, but I couldn’t finish it. [Past tense, continuing.]  
> I wrote the report yesterday. [Past tense, finished.]  
> I am writing another report today. [Present tense, continuing.]  
> I will write a final report tomorrow. [Future tense, finished tomorrow.]  
> By next week, the executive will have reviewed the report. [Future perfect tense.]  

Verbs like *will* change the form of the main verb. Added verbs are *auxiliary* verbs.

## The ‘mood’ of a verb conveys meaning

You can use verbs to describe a fact, express a wish, or make a command or request. This is called *mood*.

### Indicative mood

Expresses simple statements or questions.

**Example**  
> Jan went into the office on Monday.  
> The commander ordered the troops to stand at ease.  
> Was the report tabled in July this year?  

### Imperative mood

Expresses urgency, commands, pleas and requests. It is direct and works well for instructions.

**Example**  
> Apply now.  
> Lodge your submission by 31 July.  
> Verify your corporate credit card.  
> Please speak up.  

Be aware that imperative commands can seem blunt, even with *please*.

### Subjunctive mood

Expresses possibility, hypotheticals or desirability.

**Example**  
> If supply were to stop, we wouldn’t be able to meet demand.  
> If only they would update the data!  
> You should apply now.  

The subjunctive can work with other words to express a condition.

**Example**  
> If you took a sample, you would have some data.  
> If you applied now, we could process your application tomorrow.  

Recommendations can also use this form.

**Example**  
> That the Minister meet with peak body representatives.  

Auxiliary verbs can convey ability, possibility, permission or obligation:

- can  
- could  
- may  
- might  
- will  
- would  
- shall  
- should  
- must  
- ought to  

**Example**  
> This could take some time.  
> You must submit your application by close of business.

## Phrasal verbs need a preposition or adverb

Phrasal verbs combine a verb with one or more prepositions, or with an adverb.

**Example**  
> We need to check in with each other. [‘Check’ + ‘in’ + ‘with’.]  
> They found out 6 breaches had occurred. [‘Found’ + ‘out’.]  

When a phrasal verb needs an object, the object can go between the parts.

**Example**  
> The clue gave away the answer.  
> The clue gave the answer away.
