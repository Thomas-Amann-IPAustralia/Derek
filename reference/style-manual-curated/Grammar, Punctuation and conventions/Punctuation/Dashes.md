# Dashes

Dashes show a relationship. Generally, en dashes for spans are less accessible for users than a phrase. Use spaced en dashes to set off non-essential information in sentences.

---

## Guidance

- Use the correct symbols for en dash and minus symbol  
- Use a phrase instead of an en dash for most spans of numbers  
- Follow exceptions to the general rule  
- Use an en dash for spans in technical content  
- Join nouns with an en dash to show an equal relationship  
- Rewrite to avoid joining a prefix with an en dash  
- Space en dashes in sentences to set off non-essential information  
- Use 2 em dashes for some quoted speech and deliberate omissions in text  

---

## Use the correct symbols for en dash and minus symbol

En dashes are half the width of the font height. Use them as a type of punctuation.

Don’t use an en dash instead of a minus symbol. Screen readers will read dashes as dashes, not as the minus symbol.

In Unicode, the en dash is `U+2013`. Use the entity `&ndash;` in HTML.

To make sure screen readers read the minus symbol, use the mathematical symbol for minus. In Unicode, this is `U+2212`. Use the entity `&minus;` in HTML.

Don’t confuse the dash or the minus symbol for a hyphen.

---

## Use a phrase instead of an en dash for most spans of numbers

En dashes show a span when used with numerals, such as in a financial year, a range of values or a span of time. En dashes sometimes appear in a span of words, such as in a span of days of the week.

Avoid using en dashes in most content.

Dashes can affect readability unless a user changes their screen reader’s default punctuation verbosity settings. Screen readers generally won’t read out dashes by default. This can affect people’s ability to quickly understand spans.

That is why, in general content, it’s better to use phrases for most spans of numbers. You can use en dashes in technical content, particularly if it contains a lot of spans.

### Spans in general content

Avoid using en dashes for spans in body text and headings. Instead, use:

- **‘from’** paired with **‘to’** – e.g., *from 57 to 65 years*  
- **‘between’** paired with **‘and’** – e.g., *between Monday and Friday*  

Never mix ‘from’ or ‘between’ with an en dash.

**Correct**  
> She worked from 10 to 28 January.  
> All seminars run from 10:30 am to 11:30 am.  
> Annual rainfall between 2017 and 2019 was lower than the long-term average.  
> The help desk operates between 9 am and 4 pm daily.  

**Incorrect**  
> She worked from 10–28 January.  
> All seminars run from 10:30–11:30 am.  
> Annual rainfall between 2017–2019 was lower than the long-term average.  
> The help desk operates between 9 am – 4 pm daily.

## Follow exceptions to the general rule

There are exceptions to the rule of using phrases for spans in general content. The exceptions are for particular date spans and time spans.

### Use an en dash for date spans in:

- financial years  
- calendar years  
- the titles of publications and programs  
- terms of office  
- lifespans (birth and death)  
- limited space situations.

**Note:** Date spans in headings should follow the general rule, except when the heading contains a title that has an en dash, or using words would push the character count over 70 characters (including spaces).

Only use en dashes for time spans when you have limited space.

---

## Use an en dash for a financial year

For financial years, use 4 digits for the first number and only 2 digits for the second. Join them with an **unspaced** en dash.

Use the phrases ‘financial year’ and ‘financial years’ at first mention, unless the context makes the meaning clear.

**Example**  
> The 2019–20 budget  
> The department recently published a list of gifts received during the 2022–23 financial year.

For financial years that cross centuries, use 4 digits on either side of the en dash.

---

## Use an en dash for a calendar year

For calendar years, use 4 digits for the first number and either 4 or 2 digits for the second number. Join them with an unspaced en dash.

**Example**  
> The following statistics cover the 2023–24 calendar years.

---

## Use an en dash for a date span in titles of publications and programs

Date spans often appear in titles. Use 4 digits for the first year and 2 digits for the second, unless crossing centuries.

**Example**  
> The diversity strategy 2020–25  
> The Lake Eyre basin plan 1999–2004  

---

## Use an en dash for a term of office

**Correct**  
> James Scullin (1929–1932)  

**Incorrect**  
> James Scullin (1929–32)  

---

## Use an en dash for a person’s lifespan

**Correct**  
> Caroline Chisholm (1808–1877)  

**Incorrect**  
> Caroline Chisholm (1808–77)  

---

## Use an en dash for a date span when space is limited

Allowed in display text, tables, lists, or social media.

---

## Restrict the use of an en dash for a date span in headings

Only use when:

- heading includes a title with an en dash  
- words push the count above 70 characters.

---

## Only use an en dash for a time span when space is limited

Spacing rules:

- **Spaced en dash**: when ‘am’/‘pm’ on both sides, or in 24-hour format.  
- **Unspaced en dash**: when ‘am’/‘pm’ only at end, or with ‘noon’, ‘midday’ or ‘midnight’.  

---

## Use an en dash for spans in technical content

In technical contexts with many numbers, use en dashes for number, date, and page spans. Don’t combine with ‘from’ or ‘to’.

---

## Join nouns with an en dash to show an equal relationship

Use an en dash between coordinate nouns.

**Example**  
> Murray–Darling Basin  
> Sydney–Melbourne flight  

---

## Rewrite to avoid joining a prefix with an en dash

**Example**  
> pre–Cold War policies  
> policies from before the Cold War  

---

## Space en dashes in sentences to set off non-essential information

Spaced en dashes act like commas or brackets to insert extra information.

**Example**  
> Three rivers – the Murray, Darling and Murrumbidgee – were discussed.

---

## Em dash

Em dashes are the same width as font height.

Australian Government style uses spaced en dashes for digital content. Follow this unless another style is required (e.g., for publication).

---

## Use 2 em dashes for some quoted speech and deliberate omissions in text

Shows a sudden interruption or omission.

**Example**  
> ‘Any more questions before ——’  

---

## Release notes

The digital edition recommends spaced en dashes for additional, amplifying, and parenthetical material, instead of the sixth edition’s unspaced em dash. Also advises using words instead of en dashes for number spans in some cases, for accessibility.
