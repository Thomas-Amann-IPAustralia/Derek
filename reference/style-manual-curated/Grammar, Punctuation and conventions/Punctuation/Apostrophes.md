# Apostrophes

Apostrophes show possession and contractions. Don’t use them in descriptive phrases or to make nouns and shortened forms plural.

---

## Guidance

- An apostrophe shows possession  
- Follow the possession rules for different types of nouns  
- A descriptive phrase doesn’t need an apostrophe  
- Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ to form a possessive shortened form  
- A possessive pronoun doesn’t need an apostrophe  
- Don’t use an apostrophe for an Australian place name  
- Don’t use an apostrophe for a period of time  
- An apostrophe shows a contraction  
- Don’t use an apostrophe to make a noun plural  
- An apostrophe can stand in for a sound  
- Some official names have apostrophes  

---

## An apostrophe shows possession

When the first of 2 consecutive nouns has an apostrophe, it means those nouns have a relationship. If the first noun in a noun phrase has an apostrophe, it means the noun is related to the other words in the phrase.

The type of relationship shown by the apostrophe differs, but all are known collectively as **‘possessives’** and sometimes as **‘genitives’**.

On this page, **‘possession’** or **‘possessive’** means any relationship between nouns – or between words in a noun phrase – that is shown by an apostrophe.

**Example**  
> This is Ariah’s desk.  
> We enjoy Adelaide’s music festivals.  
> They are the minister’s chief of staff.  
> It was a winter’s morning.

---

## Follow the possession rules for different types of nouns

To correctly show possession by using an apostrophe, first ask, *‘Who or what is doing the possessing?’*

The apostrophe goes straight after the noun that is the answer.

There are possession rules for using an apostrophe, according to the type of noun.

| Noun type | Rule | Examples |
|-----------|------|----------|
| Singular noun | Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ | the committee’s report<br>our department’s plan<br>the bus’s passengers<br>the kibbutz’s energy needs |
| Singular noun of 2 or more words, the last word being a plural ending in ‘s’ | Add an apostrophe only | the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ office<br>a Department of Social Services’ project<br>the United States’ representatives |
| Plural nouns that end in letter ‘s’ | Add an apostrophe only | both committees’ reports<br>the Joneses’ submission<br>the Sanchezes’ security passes |
| Plural nouns that don’t end in ‘s’ | Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ | our children’s education<br>the sheep’s wool<br>the gateaux’s boxes |
| Proper names ending in letter ‘s’ | Add an apostrophe and another ‘s’, even if you don’t pronounce that final ‘s’ | Burns’s report<br>James’s profession<br>Laos’s population<br>Louis’s supervisor<br>Jesus’s disciples |
| More than one noun: individual possession | Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ after each noun | Smith’s and Miller’s offices |
| More than one noun: joint possession | Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ after the last noun only | Smith and Miller’s report<br>Julie and Karl’s children |
| Singular compound noun | Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ after the compound | the attorney-general’s speeches<br>the owner-occupier’s accountant |
| Plural compound nouns ending in ‘s’ | Add an apostrophe after the compound | all owner-occupiers’ tax concessions |
| Plural compound nouns that don’t end in ‘s’ | Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ after the compound | the attorneys-general’s meeting |

---

## A descriptive phrase doesn’t need an apostrophe

Some nouns are descriptive rather than possessive. In a descriptive noun phrase, the first noun modifies the second noun by operating as a definitive adjective.

Don’t use an apostrophe for these nouns.

The first noun is essential to the meaning of the second noun. Never separate the nouns in a descriptive phrase.

**Example**  
> workers compensation [A type of insurance]  
> visitors book [A type of book]  
> drivers licence [A type of licence]

Only add an apostrophe when showing possession.

**Example**  
> They signed the visitors book. [Descriptive]  
> Her visitor’s book was lying on the table. [Possessive]  
> She attended a directors meeting. [Descriptive]  
> The director’s meetings don’t have an agenda. [Possessive]  
> You must hold a valid drivers licence. [Descriptive]  
> The officer asked to see the driver’s licence. [Possessive]  
> Each state administers workers compensation. [Descriptive]  
> Our workers’ compensation for overtime is time off in lieu. [Possessive]

*Note:* The Style Manual recognises ‘workers compensation’ as descriptive. Some agencies may use an apostrophe in this phrase.

---

## Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ to form a possessive shortened form

There are 4 types of shortened forms: abbreviations, contractions, acronyms and initialisms.

Add an apostrophe and ‘s’ to show possession.

**Example**  
> Tassie’s new permit system [Abbreviation]  
> ASIO’s files [Acronym]  
> Cth’s Act [Contraction]  
> ABS’s work plan [Initialism]

**Consider:** Can a descriptive phrase replace the possessive form?  
> ASIO files  
> Qantas aircraft  
> US data use agreements  
> ABS work plans

---

### Add ‘s’ to make a plural shortened form – not apostrophe and ‘s’

Correct:  
> MPs, LGAs, PCs  

Incorrect:  
> MP’s, LGA’s, PC’s  

Add an apostrophe to plural shortened forms to show possession:  
> MPs’ entitlements  
> POWs’ repatriation

---

### Don’t use a possessive when defining a shortened form

Correct:  
> The Department of Veterans’ Affairs (DVA) … DVA supports …  
> Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) … ASIC created …

Incorrect:  
> Department of Veterans’ Affairs’ (DVA’s) website …  
> Australian Securities and Investments Commission’s (ASIC’s) Moneysmart …

---

## A possessive pronoun doesn’t need an apostrophe

Possessive pronouns and possessive determiners never take an apostrophe.

**Example**  
> This is your office. That is theirs.  
> The fault is ours.  
> Put the report in its place.  
> It was my idea not yours!

---

## Don’t use an apostrophe for an Australian place name

**Example**  
> Kings Cross  
> Mrs Macquaries Chair

---

## Don’t use an apostrophe for a period of time

Plural time periods are descriptive, not possessive.  
**Example:**  
> 6 weeks time  
> 3 months wages  

Singular time periods use an apostrophe:  
> a day’s work  
> the year’s cycle

---

## An apostrophe shows a contraction

Apostrophes indicate omitted letters.

**Example**  
> I haven’t seen the report.  
> It’s a busy day.  

*Note:* Don’t confuse *it’s* (it is/it has) with *its* (possessive).

---

## Don’t use an apostrophe to make a noun plural

Correct:  
> the 2020s, committee reports, newer 747s, fresh avocados  

Incorrect:  
> the 2020’s, committee report’s, newer 747’s, fresh avocado’s

Also, pluralise phrases without apostrophes:  
> dos and don’ts  
> ifs, ands or buts  
> ums and ahs  
> thank-yous

Single letters and digits: add apostrophe for clarity:  
> 0’s and 1’s  
> dot your i’s and cross your t’s  
> too many s’s

---

## An apostrophe can stand in for a sound

Used in transliterations to indicate a glottal stop or similar.

**Example**  
> Qur’an  
> Ge’ez

---

## Some official names have apostrophes

Follow the official spelling.

**Example**  
> National Farmers’ Federation  
> Australian Workers Union  
> Energy Ministers’ Meeting  
> Education Ministers Meeting
