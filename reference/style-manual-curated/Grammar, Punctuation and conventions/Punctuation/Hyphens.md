# Hyphens

Hyphens connect words and prefixes so meaning is clear. Refer to your organisation’s preferred dictionary when you are not sure if you need to use a hyphen for spelling.

---

## Guidance

- Follow hyphenation rules in the dictionary your organisation uses  
- Write certain prefixes with a hyphen  
- Write most suffixes without hyphens  
- Hyphenate some but not all compound words  
- Repeat words instead of using a hanging hyphen  
- Don’t hyphenate ‘-ing’ and ‘-ed’ verbs or most ‘-ly’ adverbs  

---

## Follow hyphenation rules in the dictionary your organisation uses

Hyphens clarify meaning by connecting words and parts of words into a single unit of meaning. Use hyphens to avoid ambiguity.

**Example**  
> a little used office → a little-used office  
> a unionised workforce → an un-ionised particle  

There are few firm rules about using hyphens, and dictionaries do not always agree.  
Use the dictionary your organisation recommends and follow its hyphenation practices.

Follow the accepted naming conventions for compass points, names of people and names of places.

---

### Print considerations

Don’t break short words over a line, especially:

- words of fewer than 6 letters  
- one-syllable words  
- 2-syllable words  

Break words between syllables so that the hyphen:

- is between 2 components of a compound word (`data-base`)  
- is between the base word and the suffix or prefix (`neat-ness`)  
- comes before a consonant (`fic-tion`, not `fict-ion`), unless misleading (`draw-ings`, not `dra-wings`)  

Don’t add extra hyphens when breaking URLs or email addresses — people could misread them as part of the address.

Use the part of the word before the hyphen to suggest the rest of the word.

---

## Write certain prefixes with a hyphen

Hyphens are useful in words formed with prefixes such as:

`anti-`, `auto-`, `counter-`, `extra-`, `intra-`, `re-`, `sub-`

They are especially useful for:

- doubled-up vowels  
- clarifying new words that could be confused with existing ones  

**Example**  
> re-cover [cover again] vs recover [retrieve]  
> re-creation [create anew] vs recreation [leisure]  
> re-signed [signed again] vs resigned [stepped down]  

If a prefix such as `non-`, `pre-`, `anti-` acts on more than one word and the phrase is already hyphenated, use a second hyphen.

**Example**  
> non-English-speaking countries  
> anti-harm-minimisation stance  

---

### Doubled-up vowels

Use a hyphen when the last letter of a single-syllable prefix is a vowel and the following word starts with the same vowel.

**Write this**  
> de-emphasise, pre-eminent, re-enter  

**Not this**  
> deemphasise, preeminent, reenter  

If a word is well known, this is less important. Check the dictionary.

**Write this**  
> coordinate, cooperate  

**Not this**  
> co-ordinate, co-operate  

---

### Double letters

Hyphens can link a prefix/suffix to a word to avoid a double letter or misunderstanding.

**Example**  
> multi-item vs multilateral  
> re-sign [sign again] vs resign [leave job]  

---

### Capital letters

Hyphenate when a prefix is followed by a capitalised word.

**Example**  
> un-Australian, pro-European  

---

### Vowel combinations

Don’t hyphenate if the words end/start with different vowels.

**Example**  
> prearrange, reallocate, triennial  

Use a hyphen if the prefix is attached to a single-syllable word beginning with a vowel.

**Example**  
> de-ice  

---

### Two-syllable prefixes

- Ending in a vowel (other than ‘o’) + vowel start → often hyphenated.  
  **Example:** anti-aircraft, semi-official  
- Ending in vowel ‘o’ → often no hyphen regardless.  
  **Example:** macroeconomics, retroactive  
- Ending in a consonant → rarely hyphenated.  
  **Example:** hyperlink, interactive  

---

### ‘Co-’ and ‘ex-’ prefixes

- Many words with ‘co-’ (meaning joint) have a hyphen.  
- All words with ‘ex-’ (meaning former) are hyphenated.  

**Example**  
> co-author, co-worker  
> ex-councillor, ex-president  

---

### Numbers and italics with prefixes

Use a hyphen if a prefix is followed by a number or italicised expression.

**Example**  
> post-1960  
> anti-*raskol* measures  

---

## Write most suffixes without hyphens

Attach suffixes directly to base words. Common suffixes: `-able`, `-ate`, `-ation`, `-fold`, `-ful`, `-ise`, `-ish`, `-ly`, `-ment`, `-ness`, `-y`.

**Example**  
> readable, colourful, costly  

Hyphenate:

- `-fold` when used with a numeral: **300-fold** vs **threefold**  
- `-odd` always: **150-odd competitors**  

---

## Hyphenate some but not all compound words

Compound = 2+ words carrying a new meaning.

Hyphenate when used before a noun as adjectives. Don’t hyphenate when after.

**Example**  
> up-to-date accounts vs accounts are up to date  
> small-business owners vs owners of small businesses  

---

### Compound nouns

#### Verb + adverb  
Hyphenate.  
**Example:** shake-out, make-up, go-ahead

#### Adverb + verb  
No hyphen.  
**Example:** bypass, downpour

#### Verb + noun  
Some take a hyphen — check dictionary.

| Verb + Noun type | Hyphenation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb no suffix + noun | one word | stingray |
| Verb ‘-ing’/‘-ed’ + noun | 2 words | flying doctor |
| Single-syllable noun + verb ‘-ing’/‘-ed’ | one word | stocktaking |
| Multi-syllable noun + verb ‘-ing’/‘-ed’ | hyphen or 2 words | profit-taking |

#### Noun + noun  
Often hyphenated if equal elements, rhyming, or prepositional phrase.  
**Example:** owner-driver, hocus-pocus, editor-at-large

---

### Compound adjectives

Hyphenate when:

- Two adjectives: **bitter-sweet**  
- Noun + adjective: **accident-prone**  
- Adverbial phrase before noun: **dusk-to-dawn curfew**  

Don’t hyphenate:

- Adverb + adjective when adverb ends in ‘-ly’: **newly discovered**  
- When modified by very/most/etc.: **very well known diplomat**

---

### Clarification

Use hyphens to avoid ambiguity.

**Example**  
> more experienced staff vs more-experienced staff  

---

### Set phrases

Usually no hyphen unless further modified.

**Example**  
> tax office ruling vs retrospective tax-office ruling  

---

### Adjectives with verbs

- Compound adjectives with participles often hyphenated: **well-known**  
- Some are single words: **airborne, widespread**  
- Don’t hyphenate adverb + verb when adverb ends in ‘-ly’.

---

### Numbers and fractions

- Hyphenate compound adjectives with numbers: **4-part series**, **third-storey office**  
- Spell-out numbers 21–99 with hyphens: **twenty-one**  
- Hyphenate fractions: **one-half**, but **a half**

---

### Capitals, italics, quotation marks

Usually no hyphen.  
**Example:** High Court decision, ‘do or die’ attitude

---

## Compound verbs

Hyphenate adjective + noun or noun + verb: **cold-shoulder**, **gift-wrap**  
Don’t hyphenate adverb + verb: **bypass**, **overreact**

---

## Compound adverbs

Write as one word: **barefoot**, **downstream**

---

## Repeat words instead of using a hanging hyphen

Hanging hyphen example: **full- and part-time positions** → better as **full-time and part-time positions**

---

## Don’t hyphenate ‘-ing’ and ‘-ed’ verbs or most ‘-ly’ adverbs

**Correct:** a fully loaded truck  
**Incorrect:** a fully-loaded truck  

**Exceptions:** fully-fashioned, fully-fledged
